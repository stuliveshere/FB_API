\begin{abstract}
\noindent This report analyses four years of Australian political
advertising from the Facebook Ad Library (March 2020 to February 2024),
with a focus on third-party advertisers around the May 2022 federal
election. Approximately 320~GB of JSON across 4{,}500 daily snapshots
were ingested with PySpark on YARN, deduplicated, and windowed to six
months either side of election day, which reduced the corpus from
40~million to 5.7~million ads. A pandas UDF tagged candidate, party, and
government advertising via token matching against the Australian
Electoral Commission candidate roll. The remaining ads were clustered
with Latent Dirichlet Allocation and hand-labelled by category, with
topics lacking a single coherent theme tagged \texttt{mixed} and kept
for downstream analysis, leaving about 95{,}000 advocacy ads. The corpus
was then analysed in two ways: a deterministic temporal classifier based
on persistence and centroid metrics, and a hashtag-supervised
logistic-regression text classifier. Third-party advertising splits into
distinct temporal patterns, with election-only advertisers dominating by
count and Greenpeace Australia dominating the ongoing tier by spend. The
content classifier identifies advertisers whose messaging pivoted to
electoral themes around polling day even when their spend pattern did
not, with the Australian Christian Lobby showing the largest content
shift. Combining temporal and content methods is needed to classify
third-party political advertising. Neither metric alone is enough.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================
Winning elections requires reaching voters, and there are many ways to do this.
Online advertising is attractive because it allows a message to reach a wide
audience with minimal effort. Designing an ad, placing it online, and paying
the bill is easier than traditional methods such as door-knocking or physical
signage.

Political advertising is not limited to political parties. Third-party groups
also participate in election campaigns, and studying digital advertising
allows us to examine who these groups are, what they spend, and when they
choose to spend it. The Facebook Ad Library API publishes every sponsored
political post targeted at Australian users from March 2020 to February 2024,
covering three major political events: the COVID-19 pandemic response, the
May 2022 federal election, and the October 2023 Voice to Parliament
referendum. This report focuses on the 2022 federal election.

At approximately 320~GB of JSON files, the dataset cannot be analysed on a
conventional workstation. A single machine cannot hold it in memory, and the
joins required for deduplication and classification would be prohibitively
slow. Distributed analytics frameworks are the natural fit. PySpark is well
suited to this task because it distributes both storage and computation
across a cluster, loads data lazily, and its DataFrame API supports
efficient analysis across thousands of files. We also leverage Spark
MLlib for both the unsupervised topic modelling (LDA) and the supervised
text classifier (logistic regression with TF--IDF features).

The investigation was split into three steps: data ingestion and preparation,
exploratory analysis, and election-window analysis. These are discussed in
the following sections.

\subsection{Data ingestion and preparation}

The data consists of approximately 4{,}500 JSON files containing around 40
million rows spanning February 2020 to March 2024. Each file is a snapshot of
all Australian political advertisements active at that moment, so the same
ad appears in multiple files. Each row represents a single ad with around 25
columns covering the ad purchaser, Facebook page, creative text, spend, and
impressions. The schema varies over time, with a notable change in
June~2022 and another in August~2023; data becomes sparse after August~2023.

A null-fraction analysis was performed to visualise the schema drift.
Automatic schema inference collected the superset of all fields across the
dataset, and the per-column null fraction by month was plotted as a heatmap
(Figure~\ref{fig:null-heatmap}). Columns were then modified and merged to
produce a consistent schema across the full date range. The numeric fields
were stored as nested structs of string-typed lower and upper bounds; these
were cast to numeric types and a midpoint was computed for each field,
allowing downstream aggregations to work on a single value.

Deduplication was achieved by extracting the snapshot date from each
filename and using it to sort snapshots chronologically, grouped by ad ID. A
\texttt{row\_number()} window operation tagged each row with an
\texttt{ad\_seq\_no} where \texttt{1} indicates the most recent snapshot per
ad. Filenames used inconsistent date conventions (some 7-digit, some
8-digit) and required special handling. The cleaned data was windowed to
six months either side of the May 2022 federal election and written as
Parquet to HDFS, reducing the corpus from approximately 40~million to
5.7~million rows.

\subsection{Identifying government advertising}

To focus on third-party organisations the government advertising had to be
removed. This included political-party advertisements and candidate
advertisements. The 2022 House of Representatives and Senate candidate
rolls were sourced from the Australian Electoral Commission. A straight
join was not practical because page names can be misrepresentative and
the byline can be paid by an aide. A pandas UDF was written instead,
using Arrow to batch data between the JVM and Python workers for a 3 to
10 times speed-up over a row-by-row UDF. The UDF applied three rules in
order with the first match winning, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:match-rules}.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lX}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Rule} & \textbf{Logic and output} \\
    \midrule
    Candidate name
      & The candidate's tokenised name (surname plus first given name)
      is a subset of either the page-name tokens or the byline tokens.
      Tagged as \texttt{candidate} with party from the AEC roll. \\
    Party byline signature
      & A tokenised party signature from \texttt{aec\_parties.csv} is a
      subset of the byline tokens, e.g.\ $\{$liberal, national$\}
      \rightarrow$ LNP. Signatures are tested longest-first so LNP
      matches before LP. Tagged as \texttt{party\_org}. \\
    Government byline signature
      & The byline tokens contain one of $\{$department$\}$,
      $\{$australian, government$\}$, or $\{$australian, electoral,
      commission$\}$. Tagged as \texttt{government}.
      $\{$commonwealth$\}$ was excluded to avoid collisions with
      Commonwealth Bank. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
  \caption{Three rules applied by the byline-matching pandas UDF in order,
  first match wins.}
  \label{tab:match-rules}

\subsection{Removing commercial noise via LDA}

The non-government residual still contained commercial noise. A natural
language processing pipeline was built to remove it. The pipeline used
Latent Dirichlet Allocation to cluster the residual ads
into topic groups based on the ad text body. LDA was selected because it
is unsupervised. There were no labelled examples to train on, and LDA
scales well on Spark MLlib. Text was lowercased, regex-tokenised,
stop-words removed, and vectorised with a \texttt{CountVectorizer}
(\texttt{minDF}=100, \texttt{maxDF}=0.3, \texttt{vocabSize}=5{,}000).
After tuning the number of topics on a sample, $k = 25$ gave the cleanest
separation. The resulting topics were hand-labelled with a fine label
(e.g.\ \texttt{anti\_woodside\_petition}) and a coarse category
(\texttt{climate}, \texttt{humanitarian\_rights},
\texttt{political\_advocacy}, \texttt{cost\_of\_living}, or
\texttt{mixed}). Topics without a single coherent theme were tagged
\texttt{mixed} and kept in the corpus rather than dropped, so downstream
analysis can choose whether to include them. The result was a corpus of
about 95{,}000 advocacy ads, written partitioned by category for
selective downstream reads.

\subsection{Temporal analysis: persistence and centroid bands}

The temporal analysis characterised how each advertiser's spending related
to the election. Advertisers were ranked by pre-election spend and the top
20 retained for detailed inspection. Spending was aggregated into a weekly
time series per advertiser using \texttt{date\_trunc('week', ad\_creation\_date)}.

The raw weekly series was bursty. Most advertisers buy media in short
pulses, so any summary metric on raw weekly spend was leveraged by
single-week spikes. A four-week centred rolling mean was applied per
advertiser. A hard smoothing boundary was placed at election day, with
the pre and post halves smoothed independently. This prevented a final
pre-election spike from bleeding forward into post-election weeks and
softening the cliff edge of advertisers that stopped at the election. The
raw series was kept for plotting. Only the summary metrics used the
smoothed series.

Two metrics were computed per advertiser. Persistence was the ratio of
post-election spend to pre-election spend. Centroid was the weighted-mean
week from election day, where negative values indicate spending before the
election and positive values indicate spending after.

Two metrics were needed because persistence alone produced false
positives. A campaign that trickled spend past election day, or one that
advertised on unrelated topics after, both lifted persistence without
indicating non-election alignment. Combining persistence with the centroid
distinguished these cases.

Advertisers were split into four bands as shown in Table~\ref{tab:bands}.
The bands are also plotted on a quadrant in Figure~\ref{fig:quadrant-spend}.

\subsection{Content analysis: hashtag-supervised classifier}

The temporal classifier cannot detect advertisers whose messaging pivoted
to electoral themes while their spend remained steady. A content-based
classifier was added to capture this. All hashtags were extracted from the
residual corpus with a regex and counted by band. Two reference sets were
hand-curated from the counts: about 210 political hashtags such as
\texttt{\#federalelection} and \texttt{\#morrisonmustgo}, and about 286
non-political hashtags such as \texttt{\#breakfreefromplastic} and
\texttt{\#architecture}. Each ad's text was scanned for matches against
both sets. Ads with exactly one match-type set were used as training labels:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Positive: at least one political hashtag and no non-political hashtag.
  \item Negative: at least one non-political hashtag and no political hashtag.
  \item Ambiguous or unlabelled ads were excluded from training but scored at inference.
\end{itemize}

The hashtags themselves were stripped from the text before vectorisation,
so the model had to learn from the surrounding prose rather than the
labels. A logistic-regression classifier was trained over TF-IDF features.
Logistic regression is the standard choice for high-dimensional sparse
text classification because tree ensembles underperform on sparse input.

The training set contained 2{,}599 positive ads from 259 bylines and
5{,}262 negative ads from 303 bylines. Class diversity reduced the risk
of advertiser-identity leakage. The held-out test AUC was 0.97 with 91\%
precision and 89\% recall on the positive class. A leave-one-advertiser-out
validation across the top 15 contributors gave an ad-weighted recall of
0.61. Recall was 1.00 for several top contributors (ABC Friends, First
Tier Media, the Australian Education Union's federal branch) but
collapsed to zero for Australian Parents for Climate Action when held
out, indicating the model leaned heavily on their specific vocabulary
for that one byline. Inspection of high-scoring no-hashtag ads showed
the model correctly identified political content that the upstream filter
had missed, including Family First Party and several state-election
candidates.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
% =============================================================================

The non-party, non-government advocacy ecosystem on Facebook around the
2022 election does not behave as a single block. The temporal classifier
identifies four behavioural patterns.

The \texttt{election\_only} band is the largest by count. It covers
11 of the top 20 advertisers, including Smart Voting, Solutions for
Australia, Advance Australia, the Australian Education Union, GetUp!,
It's Not A Race, Chuffed.org, Climate~200, and ABC Friends. These
advertisers ramped up before the election and stopped immediately
afterwards. Their cumulative spend curves elbow sharply at election day.
Keyword analysis confirms that this group is dominated by
election-specific language. This is the closest Australian analogue to
US PAC-style temporal behaviour.

The \texttt{transitional} band contains advertisers that tapered rather
than stopping dead. The \texttt{ongoing} band contains five year-round
advocates whose post-election rate matches their pre-election rate.
Greenpeace Australia dominates this tier at about \$1.5M of spend across
the window; the Australian Unions, Plan International Australia,
M\'edecins Sans Fronti\`eres Australia, and Save the Children Australia
each operate at roughly a third or less of Greenpeace's volume but
maintain steady year-round messaging. No top-20 advertiser fell into the
\texttt{off\_campaign} band, since Shell was filtered upstream as a
byline-tagged commercial advertiser in notebook~02.

The content classifier identifies a different signal. Among advertisers
whose spend pattern looked steady, the content classifier reveals that
some messaging pivoted toward electoral themes during the campaign
period (defined as the four weeks before and after election day). The
largest shifts are shown in Table~\ref{tab:pivot}.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Advertiser} & \textbf{Outside campaign period} & \textbf{Campaign period} & \textbf{Shift} \\
    \midrule
    Australian Christian Lobby      & 0.49 & 0.85 & $+0.35$ \\
    Amnesty International Australia & 0.35 & 0.51 & $+0.16$ \\
    M\'edecins Sans Fronti\`eres    & 0.22 & 0.35 & $+0.13$ \\
    Australia for UNHCR             & 0.18 & 0.27 & $+0.09$ \\
    Plan International Australia    & 0.23 & 0.31 & $+0.08$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Top advertisers ranked by content-score shift between the
  campaign period and the rest of the analysis window. A positive shift
  indicates a content pivot toward electoral themes.}
  \label{tab:pivot}
\end{table}

Greenpeace's content score dropped during the campaign period from 0.18
to 0.12. Their pre-election spending was focused on the Woodside gas
campaign and not on electoral messaging. Climate~200's content score is
high year-round at 0.78, so the campaign-period score of 0.85 shows no
pivot because they were already maximally political. Advance Australia's
content score moved the opposite way --- 0.81 outside the campaign
period versus 0.56 inside ($-0.25$). Their conservative culture-war
messaging ramped up after the federal election rather than during it,
which is consistent with the high-scoring Advance Australia ads dated
October 2022 surfaced by the false-positive check (Victorian state
campaign and pre-referendum content).

Neither metric alone separates the ecosystem cleanly. Temporal banding
catches cliff-edge campaigners and off-cycle outliers but cannot detect
content pivots in steady spenders. Content classification catches pivots
but cannot speak to spend volume or timing concentration. Together they
identify three patterns: PAC-style election-only campaigners visible to
both methods, year-round advocates that pivot messaging during the
campaign visible only to the content method, and year-round advocates
that stay on issue visible only to the temporal method.

The dataset scale justified PySpark. The 40-million-row deduplication via
window functions, the LDA fit on the residual corpus, and the pandas UDF
for byline matching all leveraged Spark-specific features. None of these
would have fit on a workstation in reasonable time. Hand-curation
outperformed automation at two junctures: the LDA topic labels and the
hashtag reference sets. Both were short enough for human review but
provided strong supervision signal for downstream automation.

\subsection*{Election-boundary smoothing (nb 05)}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
# Hard smoothing boundary at election day --- pre and post halves
# smoothed independently so cliff-edged advertisers do not bleed forward.
weekly['is_post'] = weekly['week'] >= ELECTION_DATE
weekly['spend_smooth'] = (weekly
    .groupby(['bylines', 'is_post'])['spend']
    .transform(lambda s: s.rolling(window=4, center=True, min_periods=1).mean()))
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection*{Hashtag-supervised labelling (nb 06)}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
pol_re = r'#(' + '|'.join(re.escape(t) for t in political_hashtags) + r')\b'
non_re = r'#(' + '|'.join(re.escape(t) for t in non_political_hashtags) + r')\b'

v3 = v3.withColumn('has_political_hashtag',     col('raw_text').rlike(pol_re))
v3 = v3.withColumn('has_non_political_hashtag', col('raw_text').rlike(non_re))

# Strip hashtags before vectorising --- model must learn from surrounding prose.
v3 = v3.withColumn('text', regexp_replace(col('raw_text'), r'#\w+', ' '))

labelled = v3.withColumn('label',
    when( col('has_political_hashtag') & ~col('has_non_political_hashtag'), 1.0)
    .when(~col('has_political_hashtag') &  col('has_non_political_hashtag'), 0.0)
    .otherwise(None))
\end{lstlisting}
