Winning elections requires reaching voters, and there are many ways to do this. Online advertising is attractive because it allows a message to reach a wide audience with minimal effort. Designing an ad, placing it online, and paying the bill is easier than traditional methods such as door knocking or physical signage.

Political advertising is not limited to political parties. Third-party groups also participate in election campaigns, and studying digital advertising allows us to examine who these groups are, what they spend, and when they choose to spend it.
The provided dataset contains Australian political ads from the Facebook platform spanning from 2020 to 2024. It offers an opportunity to identify who these third-party groups are, how their spending aligns with major political events, and what that reveals about the broader ecosystem of political influence in Australia.
The dataset covers three major political events in Australia: the government response to the COVID-19 pandemic, the federal election in May 2022, and the Voice referendum in October 2023. This report focuses on the 2022 federal election.
At approximately 320GB of JSON files, the dataset cannot be analysed on a conventional workstation. A single machine cannot hold it in memory, and the joins required for deduplication and classification would be prohibitively slow. PySpark is well suited to this task because it distributes storage and computation across a cluster, loads data lazily, and its DataFrame API supports efficient analysis across thousands of files.
The investigation was split into three steps:
Data ingestion and preparation 
Exploratory analysis 
Election window analysis 
These steps are discussed in the following sections.
Section 1: Data ingestion and preparation
The data consists of approximately 4,500 JSON files containing around 40 million rows spanning February 2020 to March 2024. Each file is a snapshot of all Australian political ads active at that moment, so the same ad appears in multiple files. Each row represents a single ad, with around 25 columns covering the ad purchaser, Facebook page, creative text, spend, and impressions. The schema varies over time, with a notable change in June 2022 and another in August 2023. Data becomes more sparse after August 2023.
A null fraction analysis was performed to visualise the schema drift. Automatic schema inference was used to collect the superset of all fields across the dataset. The null fraction within each column was then counted and plotted as a heatmap, giving a clear picture of how the schema changed over time. Columns were then modified and merged to produce a consistent schema across the full date range. Spend and impression ranges were cast to numeric types and a midpoint was computed for each field, allowing downstream aggregations to work on a single value.
Deduplication was achieved by extracting the snapshot date from each filename and using it to sort snapshots chronologically, grouped by ad ID. Filenames used inconsistent date conventions and required special handling. The most recent snapshot for each ad was retained, with its spend and impressions taken as the cumulative total for that ad. The cleaned data was then windowed to six months either side of the May 2022 federal election and exported as Parquet to the HDFS cluster. During these steps the corpus was reduced from approximately 40 million to 5.7 million rows.

In order to focus on third party organisations in the 2022 federal election official government advertising needed to be identified. This included both general political party advertisements and specific candidate advertisements. In order to achieve this the electoral candidate rolls for both the lower house and the senate were sourced from the Australian Electoral Commission, cleaned and joined with the main dataset. A pandas UDF was written which used 3 simple rules for the join:
1. Candidate name match. Each candidate's name was tokenised and flagged a row as a candidate ad if that token set was a subset of either the page-name tokens or the byline tokens. The candidates political party was then stored in a column.
2. Party-organisation byline signature. A separate lookup, aec_parties.csv, mapped tokenised party-names to AEC party codes, for example {liberal, national} ? LNP. Signatures were ordered with a greedy longest-first algorithm so that {liberal, national} linked to LNP rather than LP.
3. Government byline signature. A small set of narrow signatures ({department}, {australian, government}, {australian, electoral, commission}) flagged government department advertising. These were flagged with a generic “government” tag.
Thus government ads ended up tagged with a match_type (candidate, party_org, government) and a political_party code where applicable. Rows with a match_type of “null” contained the non-government ads required for the analysis.

After filtering the corpus consisted of approximately 150,000 unique ads. Of those ~100,000 rows were not affiliated with a political party.

The remaining 100,000 ads were not a clean corpus of non-govermnent political advertisements. The data also contained commercial noise. Thus a machine learning/natural language processing pipeline was developed which used latent Dirichlet analysis (LDA) to cluster the non-government ads into topic groups based upon the ad text body. LDA was selected as it is an unsupervised technique which makes implementing on large datasets simpler. After tuning the number of topics on a subset of the data, the resulting 20 topic clusters where then hand labelled and used to filter out the remaining unwanted rows from the corpus. At this point approximately 100,000 rows remained.


Section 2: data analysis
The goal of the analysis was to determine how each advertiser's spending related to the election, and to group advertisers by their temporal behaviour. A number of methods were tested, including a deterministic analysis of spend and linear regression classification using seeded words to try and isolate a training corpus.
Advertisers were ranked by pre-election spend and the top 20 retained for detailed analysis. For each, post-election spend and the modal topic category (climate, humanitarian_rights, political_advocacy, cost_of_living) were attached. Spending was then aggregated into a weekly time series per advertiser using date_trunc('week', ad_creation_date), producing around 150 weekly bins across the window.
The raw weekly series was very spiky. Most advertisers buy media in short pulses, so any summary metric computed on the raw series was heavily skewed by single-week spikes. A four-week centred rolling mean was applied per advertiser to smooth the series. A hard smoothing boundary was placed at election day, with the pre-election and post-election halves smoothed independently. This prevented a final pre-election spike from bleeding forward into the post-election weeks and softening the cliff-edge of advertisers that stopped at the election. The raw series was kept for plotting, so the actual shape of each campaign remained visible. Only the summary metrics used the smoothed series.
Two metrics were computed per advertiser from the smoothed series:
Persistence — post-election spend divided by pre-election spend. Captures whether activity continued after the election. 
Centroid — weighted-mean week from election day. Negative values indicate spending centred before the election, positive values after, near-zero on election week. 
Two metrics were needed because persistence alone produced too many false positives. Some campaigns trickled past election day and others advertised on unrelated topics in the post-election period, both of which lift persistence without indicating that the campaign was non-election-aligned. Combining persistence with centroid distinguished these cases.
Advertisers were then split into four bands:
election_only — persistence < 0.30 and centroid within ±16 weeks of election day. Cliff-edged campaigners. 
transitional — 0.30 ? persistence ? 0.80. Campaigns that tapered after the election rather than stopping dead. 
ongoing — persistence > 0.80. Year-round advocates whose post-election rate matches their pre-election rate. 
off_campaign — persistence < 0.30 with |centroid| > 16 weeks. Catches isolated spending events that aren't election-aligned, such as one-off corporate ads outside the campaign cycle. Neither metric alone separates this from election_only. Combining them does. 
Discussion

The election_only band is the largest, covering roughly half of the top 20. It includes Smart Voting, Solutions for Australia, Advance Australia, the Pharmacy Guild of Australia, the Australian Education Union, GetUp!, It's Not A Race, Chuffed.org, the Australian Cyber Security Centre (a false positive, see below), and Climate 200. These advertisers ramped up before the election and stopped immediately afterwards. Their cumulative spend curves elbow sharply at election day. An examination of the top keywords for these advertisers are dominated by election-specific language - references to candidates, parties, specific policies, and direct calls to vote. 
The Australian Cyber Security Centre should have been removed by the government filter in section 1. Its byline reads simply "Australian Cyber Security Centre" and does not match any of the three government signatures used 

The largest non-election spender was greenpeace, who had consistent spend right through the entire period and no obvious pre-election advertising campaign, at least based upon this metric.
